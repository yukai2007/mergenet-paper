\section{ImageNet Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Internal geometry controls}

Table~\ref{tab:geometry} separates routing controls from later optimization
probes. A historical single-seed CIFAR-100 sweep is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:cifar-spatial}; a three-seed confirmation is in
Section~\ref{sec:cifar-seeds}. At learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, the
radius-3 model reaches 78.888\%, versus 77.218\% for global routing and
78.290\% for flattened-window routing. The corresponding improvements are
$1.670\pp$ and $0.598\pp$. Their final-epoch differences are $1.672\pp$ and
$0.610\pp$, so best-checkpoint selection does not explain the ordering.
Radius 2 reaches 78.942\%, only $0.054\pp$ above radius 3. With one seed,
this does not establish an optimal radius or statistical equivalence between
the two choices.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\input{tables/geometry}
\caption{224-pixel, 150-epoch routing controls. The first four rows use the
same learning rate and base architecture. The last row is the selected
higher-rate configuration later used for 300-epoch training. All accuracies
are single-seed EMA top-1.}
\label{tab:geometry}
\end{table}

The geometric result is specific to these support policies. Radius changes
alter both spatial coverage and the number of eligible receivers. Since row
energies sum over eligible receiver scores, candidate cardinality can affect
donor selection even before transport. The ImageNet packet has no
degree-matched randomized support control. Likewise, global \mn{} is not the
original DTEM method: it shares \mn{}'s local front end, soft inference path,
final gather, and recovery operation.

Raising the learning rate to $7.5\times10^{-4}$ improves radius-3 accuracy by
$0.428\pp$ at 150 epochs. That selected configuration is the baseline for the
optimizer probes in Section~\ref{sec:probes}. A separate $\lambda=4$ run
reaches 78.366\%, but also changes local/latent depths from $6/6$ to $4/8$
and the local window from 16 to 32; it cannot be interpreted as a
compression-ratio-only experiment.

\subsection{Three-seed CIFAR confirmation}
\label{sec:cifar-seeds}

Company ImageNet weights are unavailable, so a local CIFAR-100 campaign tests
whether the routing gain repeats across seeds and whether candidate count
alone can explain it. The matrix is two selectors $\times$ four geometries
$\times$ three seeds, 24 independent 200-epoch runs at 224 pixels with
$\lambda=2$. The primary endpoint, fixed before launch, is epoch-199 EMA
top-1 on the 10,000-image test set. Training and evaluation batches are 200.
The images are native $32\times32$ CIFAR frames resized under the historical
augmentation protocol; this is not a native high-resolution test.

The four geometries are global routing, flattened window 8, radius 3, and a
degree-matched control. The last starts from the realized radius-3
donor/receiver mask after each partition and permutes receiver columns with
fixed receiver-ID priorities. Per-donor candidate counts and the receiver
degree multiset are preserved; the control is not an independent random
graph. The two selectors are the historical batch-range threshold operator
and a per-sample float32 bisection operator. Even seed 42 is a new training
run (dataloader workers differ from the August sweep).

Table~\ref{tab:cifar-seeds} and Figure~\ref{fig:cifar-seeds} show that radius 3
is the most accurate geometry under both selectors. All eighteen same-seed
radius-3 minus control differences underlying the six rows of
Table~\ref{tab:cifar-contrasts} are positive. Under the
historical selector, the mean radius-3 advantages are $+2.11\pp$ versus
global, $+1.30\pp$ versus flattened windows, and $+1.42\pp$ versus
degree-matched support. The per-sample selector preserves the same ordering,
with smaller mean gaps of $+1.63$, $+0.77$, and $+1.19\pp$. The
degree-matched gaps indicate that original-grid locality contributes beyond
candidate cardinality in this setting. The lower panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:cifar-seeds} show a persistent mid-to-late training gap rather
than a single-epoch spike. Three seeds are enough to show a
repeated sign pattern; they are not enough to treat the sample standard
deviations as population estimates, and they do not transfer ImageNet
uncertainty.

\subsection{Complete schedules and resolution adaptation}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports complete 300-epoch runs and the 384- and
512-pixel fine-tunes. At 224 pixels, \mn{} trails dense DeiT-S/8 by
$0.878\pp$ using best checkpoints and $0.812\pp$ using final checkpoints. It
exceeds DeiT-S/16 by $1.676\pp$, but the latter is a much cheaper model at
this input resolution. The higher accuracy relative to coarse patches does
not establish better efficiency.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\input{tables/main}
\caption{Complete main runs. Fine-tunes initialize from the corresponding
EMA checkpoint: 384-pixel runs from 300-epoch 224-pixel weights, and
512-pixel runs from the 384-pixel stage. The 512-pixel \mn{} run uses the
radius-5.143 384-pixel EMA file and $R=6.857$. The selected \mn{} scratch
configuration uses a higher learning rate than dense DeiT; the fine-tuning
recipe is shared. Best and final checkpoints are kept distinct; fine-tuning
best-epoch indices are relative to that stage.}
\label{tab:main}
\end{table}

After 30 epochs at 384 pixels, radius-3 \mn{} reaches 82.582\%, compared with
82.976\% for dense DeiT-S/8. Its best-checkpoint gain over its own 224-pixel
result is $1.214\pp$, while dense DeiT gains $0.730\pp$. The gap therefore
narrows to $0.394\pp$; using final checkpoints it is $0.324\pp$. Scaling the
radius to 5.143 produces 82.574\%, a difference of $-0.008\pp$ from radius 3.
This one comparison gives no evidence of a useful accuracy gain from that
particular scaling rule, but cannot rule out other radii or recipes.

A further 15 epochs at 512 pixels reach 83.068\% for dense DeiT and 82.618\%
for \mn{}. The gap is $0.450\pp$, slightly wider than at 384 pixels.
Table~\ref{tab:resolution} and Figure~\ref{fig:resolution} summarize the
series. Dense DeiT gains $0.092\pp$ from 384 to 512 pixels. MergeNet gains
$0.044\pp$ over its actual radius-5.143 parent (82.574\%); the difference
from the separately selected radius-3 384-pixel result is $0.036\pp$.
The latter is a comparison of selected configurations, not a within-branch
fine-tuning gain. The 384-pixel narrowing is therefore not the start of a
monotone closing trend.

Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows that the models reach their best 384-pixel
scores at different epochs: 7 for dense DeiT, 20 for radius 3, and 22 for
radius 5.143. Both 512-pixel runs peak at epoch 6 of 15. These observations
do not prove that the router requires three times as much adaptation, nor
that truncating a schedule would reproduce a newly scheduled shorter run.
Short terminal slopes cannot justify extrapolating another 300 training
epochs.

\subsection{Optimizer probes do not recover the dense gap}
\label{sec:probes}

The selected 150-epoch radius-3 configuration reaches 79.316\%. Before the
later probes ran, a promotion rule required a gain larger than $0.40\pp$
(above 79.716\%) to promote a configuration. This is a campaign decision
threshold, not an estimated noise level, statistical null hypothesis, or
equivalence margin. Table~\ref{tab:probes}
and Figure~\ref{fig:tuning} report the completed one-at-a-time tests.

\centering\small
\input{tables/probes}
\caption{150-epoch probes off the selected radius-3, learning-rate
$7.5\times10^{-4}$ configuration. $\Delta$ is best EMA top-1 minus 79.316.
The weight-decay run was stopped at epoch 140 by a scheduled reboot; it is
not a completed endpoint.}
\label{tab:probes}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/tuning_screen.pdf}
\caption{Completed 150-epoch optimizer probes relative to the selected
radius-3 run. The shaded interval is the campaign-defined $\pm0.40\pp$ decision
band. The weight-decay bar uses a 140-epoch snapshot.}
\label{fig:tuning}
\end{figure}

Longer warmup changes the early-training stall---the epoch-24 to 36 gain
rises from $6.99\pp$ to $22.49\pp$---but finishes $0.078\pp$ below the
selected arm. A $\lambda=1.5\to2$ curriculum finishes $0.006\pp$ below it.
Raising the learning rate to $10^{-3}$ gains $0.034\pp$. Reducing drop-path
from 0.1 to 0.07, which matches the mean per-block stochastic depth of a
single twelve-block DeiT ramp, is the largest completed movement
($+0.218\pp$) and still misses the promotion band. The incomplete
weight-decay snapshot sits $0.262\pp$ below the selected arm after 140
epochs. The remaining ten epochs have no established accuracy upper bound,
so the run cannot be classified as a completed negative result. The completed
probes provide no evidence that these particular changes recover the gap;
they do not rule out optimization effects in other recipes or at 300 epochs.

A test-time re-evaluation of the 300-epoch \mn{} checkpoint gives a related
boundary (Table~\ref{tab:inference-merge}). Using \texttt{method=none} records
81.354\% at an achieved 392-token sequence, $0.006\pp$ below the native
\mn{} evaluation of 81.360\%. Raising the evaluation budget to 523 tokens
yields 81.380\%; lowering it to 196 tokens yields 80.228\%. These are
correlated evaluations of one checkpoint. They do not show that
training-time compression is free. They do show that modest evaluation-budget
changes do not recover the dense-DeiT gap. The
\texttt{method=none} cell still achieves 392 tokens, so it is not a 784-token
dense readout of the local encoder.

\subsection{Compression after training}

Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} compares full-validation accuracy at equal final patch
counts. At 224 pixels and 392 retained patches, ToMe achieves 81.932\%,
compared with \mn{}'s re-evaluated 81.360\%. At 196 patches, the scores are
81.752\% and 80.228\%, a $1.524\pp$ difference. The latter \mn{} point changes
the budget of the $\lambda=2$ checkpoint at evaluation; it is not the separately
trained, structurally different $\lambda=4$ model.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/accuracy_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Accuracy-only budget sweeps. Left: 300-epoch 224-pixel checkpoints.
Right: 384-pixel fine-tuned checkpoints, with radius 5.143 for \mn{}.
ToMe and PiToMe require no additional training on top of the trained dense
backbone. Lines connect measured points, not confidence bands. Equal final
patch counts do not match the full computation trajectory.}
\label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

For 384-pixel fine-tuned checkpoints, ToMe reaches 82.866\% at 1,152 patches,
versus 82.562\% for \mn{}, and 82.742\% at 576 patches, versus 81.300\%.
Across all twenty matched budget points---five at native 224 pixels, five
each at 288 and 384 pixels without resolution fine-tuning, and five after
384-pixel fine-tuning---ToMe has higher recorded accuracy. The differences
range from $0.304\pp$ to $1.524\pp$. These points are correlated evaluations
of a small number of checkpoints, not twenty independent training trials.
PiToMe is also competitive, but does not exceed \mn{} at every point;
for example, at 1,152 patches after fine-tuning its 82.534\% is slightly below
\mn{}'s 82.562\%.

Sweep values are re-evaluations and need not equal the training summary
exactly. For example, the native 224-pixel \mn{} sweep records 81.360\%
versus a training best of 81.368\%. We preserve both measurements rather than
rounding them into agreement. Repeated PiToMe evaluations also differ by up
to $0.034\pp$ in the supplied record. This is variation in repeated evaluation,
not a measured across-seed training variance or a universal noise threshold.
Most importantly, these accuracy results alone do not establish a joint
accuracy--latency advantage; Section~\ref{sec:systems} explains the harness
mismatch that prevents that conclusion.
